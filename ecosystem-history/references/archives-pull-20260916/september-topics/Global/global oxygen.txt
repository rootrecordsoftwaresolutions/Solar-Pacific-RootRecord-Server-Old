Global atmospheric oxygen currently hovers at a stable ~20.95%. However, oxygen in the air is very slowly decreasing due to fossil fuel combustion and deforestation. In contrast, aquatic and marine ecosystems are facing more severe, localized depletion due to climate change warming the water and reducing oxygen solubility.Atmospheric OxygenCurrent Levels: The volume fraction of oxygen in Earth's dry air is stable at ~20.946%.Historical Changes: Data from the Scripps O2 Program indicates atmospheric oxygen is very slowly declining (around -600 per meg since the mid-1980s) due to the burning of fossil fuels and the reduction of oxygen-producing vegetation.Historical Context: Over the last 500 million years, oxygen levels have wildly fluctuated between 10% and 35%. Earth's current breathable oxygen is the result of billions of years of photosynthesis by marine organisms, which generate about 70% of the world's oxygen.Aquatic & Marine DeoxygenationGlobal Oceans: Since the 1950s, the ocean has lost about 2% of its dissolved oxygen, with projections predicting a 1% to 7% loss by 2100. Tropical oceans and mid-ocean depth zones can see deoxygenation rates as high as 40-50% in localized areas.Rivers and Streams: A major study found that nearly 80% of over 21,000 river systems worldwide are experiencing declining dissolved oxygen levels. Tropical rivers are being hit the hardest, largely because warmer water holds less dissolved oxygen.Dead Zones: Eutrophication (nutrient pollution from agricultural runoff) frequently causes toxic algal blooms, resulting in suffocating "dead zones" in coastal areas and large lakes.
